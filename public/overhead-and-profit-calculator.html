Why overheads matter when you price a job

A day rate that pays your wages can still lose money if it does not also cover the van, insurance, tools and admin that carry on whether or not you are working. Spread those costs over the days you really bill, then build them into every price. If you only count the days you are available, the overhead per day comes out too low.

Profit is a separate layer on top. The calculator uses profit margin, which is the share of the final price you keep, because that is the number that stays true when materials and labour change. For more detail, read how to calculate construction overheads and how to calculate your profit margin.

Questions about overheads and profit

How do I work out my overheads per day?

Add up what it costs to run your business for a year, such as van costs, insurance, tools, phone, software and accountancy. Divide that by the number of days you realistically bill customers. The result is the overhead each billable day has to recover.

Which overheads should I include?

Include the costs that continue whether or not you have a job on: vehicle running costs and finance, insurance, tools and equipment, phone and software, accountancy, training and admin. Costs for one specific job, such as materials or a skip, go in that job instead.

How many billable days should I use?

Use the days you expect to invoice customers, not every day you are available. Holidays, sickness, bad weather, quoting and admin days all reduce it. A lower number gives a higher overhead per day, so it is safer to be realistic.

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Markup is a percentage added to your cost. Margin is the percentage of the selling price that is profit. A 20% markup on £1,000 of costs gives a £1,200 price, but profit is only 16.7% of that price. This calculator uses margin, so a 15% margin needs a price of cost divided by 0.85.

Does the calculator include VAT?

No. The price shown is before VAT. If you are VAT registered, add VAT to the price you quote.
